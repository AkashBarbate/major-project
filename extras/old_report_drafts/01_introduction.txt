CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
========================

1.1 Background
--------------

Every day, thousands of news articles are published across digital platforms, covering everything from politics and business to sports and entertainment. For the average reader, keeping track of this flood is nearly impossible without automated tools that can sort, filter, and flag content. News aggregators like Google News, Inshorts, and Apple News do attempt this, but almost all of them share a common limitation: they assign exactly one category to each article.

In practice, news rarely fits into a single box. An article reporting on a government policy to fund STEM research in universities simultaneously touches Politics, Education, and Science and Technology. A report on a cricket board's financial audit falls under both Sports and Business. When a system forces a single label, it inevitably hides the article from readers who are interested in the other relevant categories. This mismatch between how news is written and how it is organized is the first problem our project sets out to solve.

The second problem is credibility. The internet has made publishing effortless, and professional-looking articles can come from virtually anyone, including outlets with no editorial oversight. Sensationalized headlines, excessive use of capital letters, emotional vocabulary, and anonymous sourcing have become common tactics used to attract clicks, and readers often have no systematic way to separate reliable journalism from low-quality content. Most news platforms do nothing to surface this distinction. They present an article, file it under a category, and leave the credibility question entirely to the reader.

Our project, NewsLens, addresses both problems in a single end-to-end pipeline. It accepts a news article, either as raw text or through a URL, classifies it into one or more of eight categories, and produces a credibility score between 0 and 100.

1.2 Motivation
--------------

The idea for this project came from a frustration we experienced firsthand. While searching for news about a government scholarship program for engineering students, we noticed the article was filed only under Politics. It never appeared in our Education feed, even though students looking for scholarship information would clearly find it relevant.

We initially assumed this was a quirk of one particular app, but after testing several platforms, we realized the limitation was architectural. Every system we tried was built around a single-label classifier. An article is tagged Sports or Politics or Business, never more than one. This design works tolerably for straightforward stories, but real-world news articles are rarely that simple, and the result is a significant amount of useful content that readers interested in secondary categories never see.

The credibility angle emerged during our background reading on news datasets. We came across research from MIT showing that false news spreads on social media six times faster than accurate news, largely because sensationalized content attracts more engagement. The average reader has no practical way to evaluate whether the article they are reading comes from a rigorous editorial process or from an unreliable source. We realized that if our system was already analyzing an article deeply enough to classify it, extracting a few additional signals about trustworthiness would be a natural and genuinely useful extension.

These two motivations, improving how news is categorized and giving readers a quick credibility signal, define the scope of what NewsLens aims to do.


1.3 Problem Statement
---------------------

The project addresses two complementary challenges in modern news consumption:

Multi-label News Classification: Given a news article, the system must identify all relevant categories from a predefined set of eight labels (Politics, Science and Technology, Business and Economy, International Affairs, Education, Health, Sports, and Entertainment). Unlike conventional single-label classifiers, our approach allows each article to belong to multiple categories simultaneously, which more accurately reflects how news content is structured in the real world.

Credibility Assessment: For every classified article, the system must also produce a credibility score between 0 and 100. This score is derived from measurable characteristics of the text and its source, such as writing style, sensational language usage, and the reputation of the publishing domain. It is not a fact-checking verdict; rather, it is a heuristic estimate that helps readers quickly gauge how much trust they should place in a given article.


1.4 Objectives
--------------

The specific goals we set for this project are:

1) Build a custom, balanced news dataset by scraping articles from diverse Indian and international sources, rather than depending on publicly available datasets that tend to be outdated and category-skewed.

2) Design an LLM-assisted annotation pipeline using Google's Gemini models to generate multi-label annotations at scale, eliminating the bottleneck of manual labeling.

3) Evaluate transformer architectures and select the most suitable model for our classification task, ultimately choosing RoBERTa-base for its strong contextual understanding.

4) Fine-tune the selected model for multi-label classification using Binary Cross-Entropy loss and sigmoid activation, with techniques to handle label noise and class imbalance.

5) Apply Bayesian hyperparameter optimization through Optuna to discover the best classification threshold for each of the eight categories independently, rather than relying on a uniform 0.5 cutoff.

6) Develop a credibility scoring module that produces a 0-100 trustworthiness score based on eight hand-crafted linguistic and source-based features.

7) Integrate Explainable AI through Integrated Gradients to make the model's classification decisions transparent by highlighting which words contributed most to each predicted label.

8) Deploy the complete system as a web application with a FastAPI REST backend and an interactive Streamlit frontend, enabling real-time classification and credibility analysis.

1.5 Scope of the Project
-------------------------

The scope of NewsLens is limited to English-language text-based news articles. The system covers multi-label classification across eight predefined categories and credibility scoring based on linguistic and source-level features.

A few boundaries are worth stating explicitly. The credibility module does not perform factual verification. It does not cross-reference claims against external databases or fact-checking APIs. What it does is flag observable patterns in how an article is written and where it comes from, patterns that correlate with lower reliability. Think of it as a quick "trust signal" rather than a verdict.

The model was trained on content collected from Indian and international English-language news sources, so its accuracy may vary when applied to niche verticals like satire, legal filings, or regional-language content that has been machine-translated into English.
